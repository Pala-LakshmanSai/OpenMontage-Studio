## 1. Promise and opening

A hook states a specific adventure and gives visual evidence of the promised activity, then the footage returns to a coherent beginning. In the primary snow-cave reference, the model identifies a roughly 0:00–0:24 preview of storm, probing, trench, cave, bedding and cooking, followed by a short channel bumper. Around 0:29–1:34 it reports the host walking into the snow, probing depth and setting up tools; sampled frames should be checked for precise micro-cuts. The viewer knows both the desired result and the practical starting point.

Hooks vary. [Four days on the Alaska coast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sSsTR8qqDl4) approximately 0:00–0:14 previews beach, construction, kayaking and food while stating the goals; departure by boat and campsite scouting follow. [Rainforest hot springs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o6zb6_TTVWo) opens on the missed-pickup dilemma, then replays the journey and insertion; pickup later resolves that advance setup. [Five days in the swamp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J-sMBdJyclo) has a longer travel/family introduction: the 4fps model reports channel bumper and travel through roughly 1:05, followed by dock setup and itinerary. These are different story openings, not a compulsory hook length or evidence of measured retention.

## 2. Narrative engine and retention intent

Each task creates an immediate question answered by action and a small result, which creates the next need. In the primary example, AI analysis places probing ~0:50–1:14, marking/preparing ~1:15–1:34, digging ~1:35–2:32 and the arched entry ~2:35–3:14. Between these the host rests/eats, returns to excavation, makes a sleeping area and brings in equipment; the cave does not simply appear finished. In [three days in an Arctic shelter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iys_pmJSp9M), the model describes a snow-hidden wet obstacle on the approach (~1:04), diminishing daylight, then clearing, fire and an elevated sleeping platform. In [Arctic hot tent](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4PDLO-iC3FM), model analysis reports a cold morning prompting shelter rebuilding and a later cold-vehicle departure problem. In [no tent or sleeping bag](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p9yaDeStS7A), the model describes fire, bedding and food as linked needs; its second half reports an uncomfortable night followed by morning food and breaking camp. These linked problems and observable payoffs plausibly sustain curiosity; they are not a controlled audience-retention measurement.

The coastal camp develops around an actual water search, supplies, driftwood structure, food gathering and later camp life. The rainforest episode uses the missed pickup as the long question, while intermediate water, wet fuel, fishing and warmth create smaller needs and relief. Use decisions, intermediate results and new costs, rather than repeated shots of one unchanging motion. Do not equate a large claim with a shown consequence.

## 3. Camera, host and coverage

The host alternates direct conversational address with visible work, near hands/tools and wider location context. The sample shows handheld/selfie and POV views alongside separately placed work shots; a motivated edit can change camera position, while a single continuous camera turn must preserve physical movement and direction. Host presence is important but varies by activity and episode. Model-estimated coverage figures are not measured time allocations and do not justify a 70% face rule. Trimming should preserve real contact, task progression and result rather than keep an entire generated take because its provider returned it.

The main-reference detector's median and mean are descriptive of that particular automated pass, not an instruction to cut every few seconds. Openings may be brief montage; a cut to action detail or wider work is purposeful when it reveals what changed. Preserve the selected recurring host, raw skin, camera direction, scale, material behavior and sound perspective. Do not copy the reference presenter or reproduce their identity, voice, footage or exact dialogue.

## 4. Sound and environment

Main-reference AI analyses describe action-led natural sound—shovel and snow, clothing, wind, stove and utensils—and largely unscored work/camp passages, with music around title and outro. Other episodes use music for some intros and travel. The exact presence and placement are model judgments on mixed low-resolution sources, not an exhaustive human audit; no blanket claim that every episode is unscored follows. In this style, default to audible, plausible environmental and synchronized work sound, conversational speech tied to the moment, and no continuous music bed. A short intro or transition cue may be used when the story supports it. Sound should preserve distance and continuity and never be recycled to hide unrelated action, gibberish or a physical jump.

## 5. Setbacks, day changes and closure

The primary model analysis reports a snow-cave roof sag after the night (~13:49–14:34) and a separate storm/car recovery problem later, followed by return and home ending. The coastal-trip captions report rain-soaked pack-out around 26:32 and boat retrieval around 26:59–27:09, followed by a wrap-up. Rainforest captions report roughly five hours' waiting at 28:50 before the plane appears near 28:58; sampled ending shows return travel and family. The swamp episode offers family/guide activity, food and departure instead of a solo winter shelter template. These close the trip rather than stopping at the construction reveal.

Represent night, morning and weather as meaningful state changes: gear wetness, changed supplies, shelter condition, light, wind and ambient sound should follow the prior situation. Show a decision and its physical consequence; do not manufacture an exact temperature, timestamp or scientific age as a measured fact when it is a fictional premise.

## Canonical five-aspect shot-group vocabulary

These are observations from the cited sampled frames and audiovisual reports,
not frame-accurate measurements or a claim of a verified camera/lens model.

- **Subject:** recurring host actively doing practical work; family/guide trips add clearly distinguished participants. Preserve each production's chosen person rather than copying the reference presenter.
- **Subject Motion:** approach, inspect, unpack, measure, cut/remove material, carry, install, cook/eat, rest, diagnose a changed condition, attempt a remedy, then leave. Show the physical result and accumulated state after the action.
- **Scene:** recognizable outdoor geography and progressively developed camp; daylight, night, rain/snow and equipment condition change with the story. **Overlays separately:** occasional measurements, channel bumper/end graphics or travel map; these are not physical scenery and are not compulsory in the new style.
- **Spatial Framing:** whole-body/wide work views establish contact and scale, medium views connect the person to the task, close hands/POV reveal useful details, and direct face views explain a specific decision. The balance changes by phase; no exact measured face-time quota is established.
- **Camera:** separately placed stable work cameras alternate with handheld/selfie and POV observation, generally using a wide field of view and useful depth. Preserve normal-speed contact and motivated cuts/physical turns. Exact focal length, every movement, any time compression and all cut boundaries require source-specific evidence; never infer them from a still alone.

## Owner-directed fictional escalation

The owner separately requests **extreme, near-death-level fictional crises** for the new style and a 120-second glacier sample, with the intention of stronger retention and average view duration. This is an extension of the creative brief, not an observation that the reference creator experienced such danger and not a prediction of audience results. Construct an understandable chain: initial conditions → early sign → failed or costly attempt → worsening environmental/gear state → difficult choice → physical intervention → observable result → earned relief with consequences. Keep danger credible for the actual site and safety context; avoid arbitrary cave collapse, teleporting tools, instant shelter improvements or gratuitous narration that claims an unshown rescue. Use the selected host, exact state and plausible continuous environment throughout. A 120-second excerpt should show a complete causal crisis and payoff rather than compress a seven-day promise into an unearned montage.
