# Readable decisions and purposeful pace

Owner correction, **6 November 2026 IST**, after the Ice Leviathan remake.
Apply these general principles to narrative action in every AI-video style,
including future styles. Preserve each style's art, source, voice and sound rules.
For AI Movie, read with the [screenplay foundation](ai-movie-screenplay-foundation.md)
and [editorial playbook](ai-movie-editorial-continuity-playbook.md).

## The audience must understand the action without the project notes

A screenplay intention is a production obligation, not evidence that the footage
communicates it. For a consequential beat, identify the intended reading and its
most likely wrong reading. For example: **chooses to enter danger to save someone**
versus **slips, is pushed, or is pulled into danger**. Establish enough visible
evidence to distinguish them before the irreversible action. Use what fits the
scene: notice a target, an eyeline, a reaction, a preparatory movement, an offered
hand, a change of course, or a brief natural line. Do not add explanatory dialogue
automatically or prescribe a separate close-up for every thought.

Stage the decisive movement and its result in a readable view. A body leaning
over an edge followed by a body underwater does not inherently show a chosen
dive. Distinguish an earlier accidental knockdown from a later voluntary entry;
preserve any needed recovery, orientation and self-initiated movement. Do not let
a camera cut, spray, darkness, crop or convenient offscreen interval hide the
very event that gives the scene its meaning. If the audience is intentionally
denied that event for a mystery, establish the uncertainty as deliberate and pay
it off; do not accidentally obscure the protagonist's ordinary decisions.

Apply this equally to attacks versus accidents, surrender versus capture, rescue
versus abduction, a gift versus a dropped object, permission versus coercion,
boarding versus already being aboard, and sacrifice versus an unexplained death.
The concrete dive example does not limit the rule.

## Continue the physical action and its meaning

Plan the necessary parts of **stimulus, decision, attempt, contact/means, result
and response** in existing scene fields. Show only the phases needed for this
audience to follow this action. Keep the actor, target, approach, support, load,
direction and destination legible across the selected views. Follow consequential
supporting action: an arriving rescuer, a braced helper, a waiting group or a person
left holding a child cannot reset at the next generation boundary.

Each completed event advances the inherited state. Do not replay a disappearing
creature, repeat a fall, restore a missing person, exchange a held child silently,
or restart preparation after the result has already occurred. Inspect meaningful
background regions too. An apparently returned child or threat can undo the
foreground story even when the lead's face is correct. A crop is acceptable only
when the remaining picture still communicates the required cause and action.

A deliberate ellipsis needs an observed cue and a readily inferable result.
Routine walking or a minor cloth loan may be omitted when clear and expendable.
Do not classify an unseen decisive choice, water entry, first rescue contact,
release of a supported load, or irreversible outcome as routine travel merely
because the provider did not return it. Redesign unsubmitted staging or make a
targeted compatible pickup within authority. Preserve refusals and no-replay rules.

## Direct everyone visible, including the silent people

Owner elaboration, 6 November 2026 IST: emotional connection depends on readable
actions **and emotions across the whole frame**, not just the lead and current
speaker. An emergency does not stop affecting a parent, helper, witness or group
when the camera focuses on someone else. They retain their own knowledge, goal,
attention, position, support and ongoing task across cuts.

Use the existing scene/bible fields to give relevant visible people or coherent
background groups a compact performance direction:

| Track | Direction the footage must carry |
|---|---|
| Knowledge and objective | What this person knows now, wants, fears, or conceals; who/what has their attention |
| Physical entry state | Location, standing/kneeling/seated posture, support, held objects and current task |
| Stimulus and behavior | What changes, what they notice, and the purposeful action or response it causes |
| Emotional progression | A playable change expressed through gaze, face, breath, posture, voice or interaction |
| Exit state | Where/how they remain, what they hold, and which action/emotion the next view inherits |

Do not invent elaborate acting for every distant extra. Assign useful group roles
and stage manageable interactions. A helper braces, another brings a rope, a
parent tries to reach the child or is restrained for safety, a witness summons
help. Their behavior follows the event and their character, rather than everyone
staring, posing, waiting for a line or performing identical panic gestures.

An actor can be deliberately still, injured, stunned, listening, refusing to help
or hiding guilt. Make that state legible and let it develop when the event changes.
For example, a failed reach, interrupted breath, fixed attention on the last known
position, a hand closing on an empty mitten, or an aborted appeal can express loss
without a speech. Choose a few appropriate behaviors, not this entire list. Silence
is valid; an unchanged neutral portrait is not evidence of grief, shock or guilt.
Do not impose screaming, tears, constant movement or melodrama on everyone.

Do not leave a character frozen because a previous prompt ended with "freezes" or
"unable to speak." Direct what happens next to that response. A passive lead while
helpers struggle needs an intelligible role, limitation, refusal, conflict or
reaction. More camera movement cannot substitute for a person doing or feeling
something relevant. References should support the current interaction rather than
import a posed, static portrait as the whole performance.

Standing, kneeling, sitting, facing, reaching and supported weight are continuing
states. Show the meaningful change, preserve it through a motivated cut, or make
a deliberate temporal ellipsis legible. Do not silently reset a background person's
pose or location between adjacent views. Inspect the relevant background as well
as the featured face when selecting a true continuation.

For a scene's emotional turning point, ask what an unfamiliar viewer can perceive:
**what happened, who understands it, what each affected person feels, and how that
changes their next action**. Inspect the actual expression/interaction and selected
timing. A label such as "devastated," a scripted line or music cannot establish that
the returned performance conveys it. A deliberately withheld fact may remain a
mystery; the characters' observable response still has to make sense.

## Continuity is not slow literal coverage

The owner wants **clear, engaging flow**, without abrupt missing causes or boring
overdevelopment. Flexible runtime is permission to use necessary time, not an
instruction to expand the film. A longer export is not evidence of better pacing.
Cut redundant material before extending the runtime or buying more coverage.

Every retained passage needs a live dramatic job: new information, progress,
resistance, a changed tactic, a decision, a relationship change, suspense that
develops, a meaningful reaction or an earned emotional landing. Quiet scenes may
breathe when expression or anticipation develops. Remove repeated explanation,
duplicate reveals, idle approach/departure tails, repeated helplessness, staged
waiting and poses retained merely to fill a 30-second request.

Give an urgent action believable urgency. Repeating wide, face and object views
with no new change can stall a rescue even if every individual shot is attractive.
Use one readable take or concise connected coverage; protect the decision and
physical completion, then move forward. Vary rhythm with the dramatic situation.
There is no universal shot count, maximum take length, face percentage or required
pause. Do not accelerate ordinary movement, cut complete native speech, or replace
scene development with rapid random inserts. The actual two-second minimum is a
floor, not proof of clarity, good pacing, or a target duration for every shot.

## Time cues for every AI Movie

Owner instruction, 6 November 2026 IST: use necessary time/chapter cues in **any
AI Movie** so viewers remain oriented and emotionally connected. This authorizes
purposeful cues such as "Seven years later," "That night," or "Ten winters after
the flood" when appropriate; it does not authorize unrelated explanatory captions.
Preserve other named styles' distinct text rules.

Plan chronology in the existing screenplay before generation: event order,
elapsed time, ages/season/state changes, flashback entry and return, and the scene
where each change must first be understood. Decide whether picture/sound already
makes the transition clear. Add a concise cue when a meaningful jump, age change,
return to an earlier event or epilogue would otherwise be confusing. Do not label
every cut, ordinary short journey or obvious continuous action.

Place the cue **before or at the opening of the first affected scene**, before
the viewer has to interpret changed ages, circumstances or relationships. Choose
deliberately between a brief separate chapter card and a restrained overlay on a
calm establishing image. A major temporal reset may benefit from a separate card;
an overlay can preserve momentum when the image has space and no competing action.
Do not interrupt a decisive movement or obscure a face, hand, clue or subtitle.
Use a distinct return cue only when needed to distinguish the present from a
flashback; do not make every memory a title sequence.

Define a small film-specific typography system: one suitable readable family,
consistent weight/case, high contrast, safe frame margins and stable placement
chosen for the composition. Review at intended output size and a smaller viewing
size. Use the film's visual world, not default tiny text or a generic flashy
template. Simple fades or cuts usually suffice; no automatic whoosh, ornate
animation, logo or oversized title. Hold from full legibility for comfortable
reading, normally around two to four seconds for a short cue, adjusted to wording
and dramatic pace. Preserve the actual two-second minimum; fade time alone is not
the readable hold. Carry or deliberately resolve the surrounding sound.

Every relative cue needs a clear time origin. After "The first winter," "Ten
winters later" may imply eleven winters after the climax. If the intended elapsed
time is ten from the climax, say "Ten winters after the flood" or establish that
origin another clear way. Reconcile wording with the ages, costume/environment,
performed scenes and source chronology. Use honest nonspecific wording if the
story does not establish a precise interval; do not fabricate dates in factual work.

Record cue wording, temporal origin, placement, readable hold, treatment and
entry/return purpose in existing script/edit metadata. Check the encoded cue at
the actual scene boundary in the same final review. Metadata and an age label
cannot substitute for a cue that the audience never sees. These cues support
clear storytelling; they do not repair a missing action or performance.

## Carry the rule through the existing pipeline

1. **Writing:** review the whole story first where the active style requires it.
   For pivotal choices, write the intended reading, likely misreading and visible
   distinction in the existing required beat/scene description. Allocate performed
   time and remove repetition. Keep the story's emotion, curiosity and payoffs.
2. **Generation:** request manageable visible action, its meaningful start/end
   states and enough useful handles. Bind compatible references. Do not mix an
   earlier state or ambiguous accident pose into the intended voluntary action.
3. **Selection:** inspect the actual returned decisive movement and relevant
   neighboring state. Record the observed evidence in the existing coverage
   artifact. A filename, prompt, successful job, editor-written reason, before/after
   still pair or minimum-shot pass cannot stand in for the missing decision.
   Record an ambiguous or unseen beat as unresolved; do not write the intended
   screenplay action into an `observed` field without seeing it.
4. **Editing:** select for changed information and complete action. Challenge full
   source takes by their dramatic work, not by imposing a quota against them.
   Where a concrete fault is found, check the short repaired passage with its
   neighbors. Do not retain a contradiction to avoid shortening the movie.
5. **Finishing:** use the existing brief moving opening, consequential passage and
   ending check to ask what a viewer unfamiliar with the script would conclude.
   Check clarity and pace together. A known ambiguous critical beat is a material
   fault. If moving review is unavailable, disclose that limit and keep the
   uncertain semantic beat open; frame samples and decoding are not a substitute
   certificate. Extra detailed review is appropriate for an explicit owner request
   or concrete failure, not a new routine per-clip paid review or approval loop.

For AI Movie and other styles with the existing narrative contract, custom FFmpeg
assembly and Downloads delivery must not bypass `validate_coverage` or the normal
edit/compose completion checks. Run the read-only equivalent when using a custom
assembler:

```bash
python -m lib.narrative_continuity path/to/project
```

It validates recorded coverage integrity, **not audiovisual meaning or audience
engagement**. A failed check cannot be relabeled a pass because rendering succeeds.
A technical preview may be retained with its unresolved status; do not call it a
finished narratively checked film. Do not fabricate observations to satisfy the
validator. No instruction or test can guarantee perfect future footage.
