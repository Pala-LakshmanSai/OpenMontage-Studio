## Working principles for a new winter story

- Begin with a concrete, observable need and let it unfold through causally linked tasks. Preserve setup, manipulation, changed state and payoff; allow real work to take screen time.
- Establish one map of the dwelling, hearth, door, work table, woodpile, snow and paths. Keep the same identities, wardrobe, props, light sources and temporal/weather state; record before/after state per shot.
- Choose a plausible human camera position, then cover action with wide orientation, medium bodies, and selective close hands or tools. Avoid selfie/vlog language, impossible camera jumps, artificial zooms and ornamental sweeping camera moves.
- Across cuts keep action, hand direction, prop position and object state plausible; use progressions and spatial cutaways rather than forcing fake one-take morphs. Process can be compressed for two minutes but should remain understandable.
- The reference uses on-screen explanatory text and brief dialogue in the available subtitle file. Whether to add narration to the new film is a creative production choice, not a verified claim about the source. The user's requested story/narration deserves a design, but any narration should leave space for actions and not describe events that the image contradicts.
- The visual sample has textured walls, daylight, worn practical utensils and patterned textiles. This is observational detail, not a mandate to copy specific identities, dress, interiors or a desert meal into an Amish winter setting. Design a historically and culturally plausible original environment; avoid claims that fictional generated subjects are verified real people.
- Audit selected clips at natural speed and at cuts frame-by-frame for face/hand deformation, cloth/tool changes, object teleportation, spatial mismatch and unrealistic light or snow, plus sound continuity. Captions, one-second images and contact sheets are insufficient for these final gates.
